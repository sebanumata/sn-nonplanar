A nozzle that moves up and down

Pick a toolpath and watch the part build up. In planar printing the nozzle climbs in steps; with sn_non-planar it moves in X, Y and Z at the same time.

The difference

It's not a slicer. Here, you design the toolpath.

A slicer takes your part and decides on its own how to fill it with layers.
sn_non-planar works the other way around: the line the nozzle draws is the design. You choose the pattern, the rhythm and the height of every wave, and that exact path is what gets printed.

With a slicer

  • You have to model the texture into the mesh: heavy files with millions of triangles.
  • The slicer cuts it into flat layers and approximates it as best it can.
  • If it isn't a solid, it can't print it: open cells, blobs, strands in mid-air.
  • Every layer has its own seam and its own breaks.

With sn_non-planar

  • You start from a simple shape and the toolpath adds the pattern.
  • The nozzle really moves up and down within the same loop.
  • You print things that only exist as a toolpath: open lattice, blobs, bands.
  • The wall comes out as one continuous line, with no breaks and no retractions.

What you get

Pieces nobody else makes

If you sell planters, lamps or decor, you get a finish no slicer can produce. That's your edge.

You design, not configure

You decide how the surface looks with design criteria: rhythm, amplitude, openness. No fighting hundreds of settings to get it "more or less" right.

Recipes already printed

The presets come from parts that were actually printed, not from theory. And when something turns out great, you save it as your own.

It's not a general-purpose slicer: it isn't meant for mechanical parts or for slicing any STL. It's a tool for designing the toolpath.

Benefits

What changes when you stop printing flat

Two ways to stack plastic

The same profile, sliced two ways: flat layers on the left, and on the right layers that tilt little by little until they follow the edge.

Traditional · planar

Z mm · fixed within the layer

Each layer is a plane. Curves are approximated with steps (in red, the material that's missing or extra).

Non-planar · sn_non-planar

Z mm · changes at every point

The layers tilt from the floor up to the edge: the nozzle moves up and down as it travels and the surface follows the shape.

Surfaces without steps

The layers follow the curve of the part. Goodbye staircase effect on curved and sloped areas.

Layers that interlock

With wavy layers, a crack no longer finds a straight plane to run along: it has to follow a longer path that cuts across the load.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Does it print solid parts, with infill?
Not yet: this version is for vase mode only, one continuous spiral wall, no infill. Infill mode comes in a future version. Meanwhile, ideal for vases, planters, lamps and hollow pieces.
Does it work with my Bambu Lab?
Yes: A1 mini, A1, A2L, P1P, P1S, X1, X1 Carbon, X1E, P2S and H2S, plus the dual-nozzle H2D, H2D Pro, X2D and H2C, which print with just one of their nozzles. The A1 mini is the one I've tested the most; on the others, watch the machine during your first print.
How is it different from a slicer with a non-planar mode?
Those slicers start from your part and bend some layers, mostly the top ones. sn_non-planar starts from the toolpath: you design the wall pattern, line by line. That's why it does things a slicer can't, like lattice with open cells or blobs.
Do I need to know how to code or edit G-code?
No. Everything is done from a panel in Blender, with sliders and a preview of the toolpath.
Can I use it with other brands?
Yes. Slice any part for your printer in OrcaSlicer, PrusaSlicer, Cura or Creality Print and load that G-code: the add-on reads the firmware, bed, max height, nozzle and start/end G-code from it. It works with Marlin, Klipper and RepRapFirmware and 1.75 mm filament; it's not for delta printers. The one I've tested the most is the A1 mini: on other brands, watch the machine during your first print.
